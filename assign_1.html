Digital Grainer is an online edition of the poem The Sugar-Cane. The 1764 version of this poem is the main primary source for the site, along with artwork from Vanessa Lee on the homepage and other art from John Carter Brown Library's Archive of Early American Images. Two of the many secondary sources specifically mentioned in the about page are John Gilmore's 1999 Poetics of Empire, which is a scholarly edition of The Sugar-Cane, and Thomas W. Krise's 1999 Caribbeana, which is an anthology of 17th and 18th century writing about the Caribbean. These two works have been pivotal in the creation of the site and its creator's understanding of the poem. These two works are referenced in expository text at the top of each “page” in the page-by-page version of the poem on the site. A large collection of other primary and secondary sources were used as well, all of which are available on the site's bibliography page, which is linked on the home page.

The creators wanted to create an easily accessible online edition of James Grainer's The Sugar-Cane for use in graduate and undergraduate teaching. This text was selected because it covers the topic of slavery in Caribbean plantations, which has seen a surge in scholarly interest in recent years. The secondary sources were used to give both the creators and readers of the site insights into the poem's text.

To bring the poem from print to computer, the creators transcribed the poem's text and digitized artwork, though I wasn't able to find the exact tools they used to do this. The main tool the creators used for the display of this project was Ed, which is a Jekyll theme for GetHub. Ed is based on minimal computing principles, meaning a reduced size and complexity of the site so it's more accessible to users. This simplicity also improves accessibility by being easier to load regardless of internet speed. The site is also set up so there are 2 main versions of the text - one that can be read page-by-page, just as it would have been in 1764, and the other as a full text all at once. The full text version allows readers to both save the text as a pdf and search within the entire text using a search function.

I can clearly see the amount of thought that went into the creation of this website. The creators intentions are evident in every feature, from the ability to annotate the page, to the guiding explanations at the top of each page in the “Counter-Plantation” (page-by-page version of the text,) to the bibliography listing dozens of extra consulted sources. If I was studying this text in college or grad school I think this source would be among the holy grail of finds. Additionally, the creme colored background and grey text make reading the page incredibly easy. The wide margins and minimalistic page design also help with readability on a laptop screen. The one thing I don't love about the site is that that About page is only accessible from the home page, and not available on the side menu, but that, of course, is a minor gripe on an otherwise lovely project.

In this reflection I continually reference “the creators” without saying who those individuals actually are. They are all listed at the bottom of the “about” page and I would like to recognize them by name here. The creators of this digital edition of The Sugar-Cane are Elizabeth Cornell, Stephen Fragano, Alex Gil, Lina Jiang, Julie Chun Kim, Vanessa Lee, Cristobal Silva, Kimberly Takahata, and Ami Yoon. These people have worked together to create a truly amazing digitized version of a historically significant poem, increasing its accessibility in the modern day.
